The section on the four noble truths has been expanded with material mostly drawn from <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn141/en/sujato'>MN 141</a>. These discourses are the most influential texts for modern Theravada meditation, prompting countless modern commentaries. Comparative study of the several parallel versions reveals that this discourse, while comprised almost entirely of early material, was compiled in this form as one of the latest texts in the Pali suttas (see <a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn47.18/en/sujato'>SN 47.18</a> and note). | While mindfulness is always useful (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn46.53/en/sujato#15.4'>SN 46.53:15.4</a>), the “establishment of mindfulness” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>satipaṭṭhāna</i>) refers especially to a conscious development of contemplative practices based on mindfulness, i.e. “mindfulness meditation”.

## dn22:1.7  `ms7D_942, msdiv373`
- **PLI**: “Ekāyano ayaṁ, bhikkhave, maggo sattānaṁ visuddhiyā, sokaparidevānaṁ samatikkamāya dukkhadomanassānaṁ atthaṅgamāya ñāyassa adhigamāya nibbānassa sacchikiriyāya, yadidaṁ cattāro satipaṭṭhānā.
- **EN**: “Mendicants, the four kinds of mindfulness meditation are the path to convergence. They are in order to purify sentient beings, to get past sorrow and crying, to make an end of pain and sadness, to discover the system, and to realize extinguishment.
- **NOTE**: The phrase <i lang='pi' translate='no'>ekāyano maggo</i> (“path to convergence”) is given multiple meanings in commentaries and ancient Chinese translations. Outside of <i lang='pi' translate='no'>satipaṭṭhāna</i>, it is used in only one context in Pali, where it means to “come together with” (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn12/en/sujato#37.5'>MN 12:37.5</a>). At <a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn47.18/en/sujato#3.4'>SN 47.18:3.4</a> the phrase is spoken by Brahmā, which suggests it was a Brahmanical term. Yajñavalkya—in a core passage full of details shared with the suttas—uses it for a place where things unite or converge (Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 2.4.11; in the same sense also at Chāndogya Upaniṣad 7.5.2; Mahābhārata 14.19.1–2). Thus <i lang='pi' translate='no'>satipaṭṭhāna</i> leads to everything “coming together as one”. In other words, as seventh factor of the noble eightfold path, it leads to <i lang='pi' translate='no'>samādhi</i>, the eighth factor (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn45.1/en/sujato#3.9'>SN 45.1:3.9</a>; see also <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn44/en/sujato#12.3'>MN 44:12.3</a>). <i lang='pi' translate='no'>Ekāyana</i> is also a name for a certain Sanskrit scripture (in a list of texts at Chāndogya Upaniṣad 7.1.2, 7.1.4, 7.2.1, 7.7.1; Puruṣottamasaṁhitā 1.10; Praśnasaṁhitā 2.38). Now, the latter text discusses the <i lang='pi' translate='no'>ekāyana</i> as <i lang='pi' translate='no'>nivṛtti</i>, “renunciation”, which is, in fact, introduced to the Vedic system by Yajñavalkya in the above-cited passage. Thus as a scripture it probably originally referred to that very passage exemplifying the Upaniṣadic teachings, which are otherwise omitted from the Chāndogya list.

## dn22:1.9
- **PLI**: Idha, bhikkhave, bhikkhu kāye kāyānupassī viharati ātāpī sampajāno satimā vineyya loke abhijjhādomanassaṁ,
- **EN**: It’s when a mendicant meditates by observing an aspect of the body—keen, aware, and mindful, rid of covetousness and displeasure for the world.
- **NOTE**: The idiom <i lang='pi' translate='no'>kāye kāyānupassī</i>, literally “observes a body in the body” refers to focusing on a specific aspect of embodied experience, such as the breath, the postures, etc. | “Keen” (or “ardent”, <i lang='pi' translate='no'>ātāpī</i>) implies effort, while “aware” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>sampajāno</i>) is the wisdom of understanding situation and context. | “Covetousness and displeasure” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>abhijjhādomanassaṁ</i>) are the strong forms of desire and aversion that are overcome by sense restraint in preparation for meditation.

## dn22:1.10
- **PLI**: vedanāsu vedanānupassī viharati ātāpī sampajāno satimā vineyya loke abhijjhādomanassaṁ,
- **EN**: They meditate observing an aspect of feelings—keen, aware, and mindful, rid of covetousness and displeasure for the world.
- **NOTE**: “Feelings” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>vedanā</i>) are the basic tones of pleasant, painful, or neutral, not the complexes we call “emotions”.

## dn22:1.11
- **PLI**: citte cittānupassī viharati ātāpī sampajāno satimā vineyya loke abhijjhādomanassaṁ,
- **EN**: They meditate observing an aspect of the mind—keen, aware, and mindful, rid of covetousness and displeasure for the world.
- **NOTE**: “Mind” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>citta</i>) is simple awareness. In meditation contexts, “mind” is often similar in meaning to <i lang='pi' translate='no'>samādhi</i>.

## dn22:1.12
- **PLI**: dhammesu dhammānupassī viharati ātāpī sampajāno satimā vineyya loke abhijjhādomanassaṁ.
- **EN**: They meditate observing an aspect of principles—keen, aware, and mindful, rid of covetousness and displeasure for the world.
- **NOTE**: “Principles” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>dhammā</i>) are the natural “systems” of cause and effect that underlie the “teachings”. The renderings “mind objects” or “mental qualities” are incorrect, as many of the things spoken of in this section are neither mind objects nor mental qualities. “Phenomena” is a possible translation, but the emphasis is not on the “appearance” of things, but on the “principles” governing their conditional relations.

## dn22:2.1  `bj8.438, cck10.341, dr10.322, ms7D_945, msdiv374, pts-cs2, pts-vp-pli2.291, vri2.215`
- **PLI**: Kathañca pana, bhikkhave, bhikkhu kāye kāyānupassī viharati?
- **EN**: And how does a mendicant meditate observing an aspect of the body?

## dn22:2.2
- **PLI**: Idha, bhikkhave, bhikkhu araññagato vā rukkhamūlagato vā suññāgāragato vā nisīdati pallaṅkaṁ ābhujitvā ujuṁ kāyaṁ paṇidhāya parimukhaṁ satiṁ upaṭṭhapetvā.
- **EN**: It’s when a mendicant—gone to a wilderness, or to the root of a tree, or to an empty hut—sits down cross-legged, sets their body straight, and brings mindfulness to the present.
- **NOTE**: The context here—a mendicant gone to the forest—establishes that this practice takes place in the wider context of the Gradual Training. Indeed, this whole sutta can be understood as an expansion of this phrase, mentioned briefly at <a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn2/en/sujato#67.3'>DN 2:67.3</a>.

## dn22:2.3
- **PLI**: So satova assasati, satova passasati.
- **EN**: Just mindful, they breathe in. Mindful, they breathe out.
- **NOTE**: The most fundamental meditation instruction in Buddhism. Notice how the Buddha phrases it: not “concentrate on the breath” as an object, but rather “breathing” as an activity to which one brings mindfulness.

## dn22:2.4  `mc10.249, ms7D_946, sya10.326`
- **PLI**: Dīghaṁ vā assasanto ‘dīghaṁ assasāmī’ti pajānāti, dīghaṁ vā passasanto ‘dīghaṁ passasāmī’ti pajānāti.
- **EN**: Breathing in heavily they know: ‘I’m breathing in heavily.’ Breathing out heavily they know: ‘I’m breathing out heavily.’
- **NOTE**: The stages of breath meditation are not meant to be done deliberately, but to be observed and understood as the natural process of deepening meditation. When starting out, the breath is somewhat rough and coarse.

## dn22:2.5  `ms7D_947`
- **PLI**: Rassaṁ vā assasanto ‘rassaṁ assasāmī’ti pajānāti, rassaṁ vā passasanto ‘rassaṁ passasāmī’ti pajānāti.
- **EN**: Breathing in lightly they know: ‘I’m breathing in lightly.’ Breathing out lightly they know: ‘I’m breathing out lightly.’
- **NOTE**: Over time, the breath becomes more subtle and soft.

## dn22:2.6  `csp1ed7.232, csp2ed7.232, ms7D_948`
- **PLI**: ‘Sabbakāyapaṭisaṁvedī assasissāmī’ti sikkhati, ‘sabbakāyapaṭisaṁvedī passasissāmī’ti sikkhati.
- **EN**: They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe in experiencing the whole body.’ They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe out experiencing the whole body.’
- **NOTE**: Contextually the idiom “whole body” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>sabbakāya</i>) here refers to the breath, marking the fuller and more continuous awareness that arises with tranquility. Some practitioners, however, interpret it as the “whole physical body”, broadening awareness to encompass the movement and settling of energies throughout the body.

## dn22:2.7  `ms7D_949`
- **PLI**: ‘Passambhayaṁ kāyasaṅkhāraṁ assasissāmī’ti sikkhati, ‘passambhayaṁ kāyasaṅkhāraṁ passasissāmī’ti sikkhati.
- **EN**: They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe in stilling the physical process.’ They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe out stilling the physical process.’
- **NOTE**: The “physical process” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>kāyasaṅkhāraṁ</i>) is the breath (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn41.6/en/sujato#1.8'>SN 41.6:1.8</a>).

## dn22:2.11  `bj8.440, dr10.323, ms7D_951, pts-vp-pli2.292, sya10.327`
- **PLI**: Iti ajjhattaṁ vā kāye kāyānupassī viharati, bahiddhā vā kāye kāyānupassī viharati, ajjhattabahiddhā vā kāye kāyānupassī viharati.
- **EN**: And so they meditate observing an aspect of the body internally, externally, and both internally and externally.
- **NOTE**: “Internally” is one’s own body, “externally” the bodies of others, or external physical phenomena. This distinction is applied broadly in Buddhist meditation, but it is more relevant in some contexts than others. In the case of the breath, one is focusing on one’s own breath, but when contemplating, say, a dead body, or the material elements, there is more of an external dimension. Starting with “me” in here and the “world” out there, this practice dissolves this distinction so that we see we are of the same nature as everything else.

## dn22:2.12
- **PLI**: Samudayadhammānupassī vā kāyasmiṁ viharati, vayadhammānupassī vā kāyasmiṁ viharati, samudayavayadhammānupassī vā kāyasmiṁ viharati.
- **EN**: With respect to the body, they meditate observing the liability to originate, to vanish, and to originate and vanish.
- **NOTE**: This is the <i lang='pi' translate='no'>vipassanā</i> (“insight” or “discernment”) dimension of meditation, observing not just the rise and fall of phenomena, but also their conditioned “nature” as being “liable” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>-dhamma</i>) to impermanence. The meaning of this passage is explained at <a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn47.42/en/sujato'>SN 47.42</a>. Apart from these passages, <i lang='pi' translate='no'>vipassanā</i> in <i lang='pi' translate='no'>satipaṭṭhāna</i> pertains specially to the observation of principles.

## dn22:2.13
- **PLI**: ‘Atthi kāyo’ti vā panassa sati paccupaṭṭhitā hoti yāvadeva ñāṇamattāya paṭissatimattāya anissito ca viharati, na ca kiñci loke upādiyati.
- **EN**: Or mindfulness is established that the body exists, to the extent necessary for knowledge and mindfulness. They meditate independent, not grasping at anything in the world.
- **NOTE**: Mindfulness meditation leads to a range of knowledges as detailed by Anuruddha at <a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn52.6/en/sujato'>SN 52.6</a> and <a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn52.11/en/sujato'>SN 52.11</a>–24. An arahant is “independent” of any attachment (eg. <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn143/en/sujato'>MN 143</a>), but <i lang='pi' translate='no'>satipaṭṭhāna</i> is also taught to give up dependency on views of the past and future (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn29/en/sujato#40.1'>DN 29:40.1</a>).
