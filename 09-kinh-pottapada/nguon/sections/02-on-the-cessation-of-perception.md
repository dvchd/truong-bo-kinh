- **EN**: Sir, a few days ago several ascetics and brahmins who follow various other religions were sitting together at the debating hall, and a discussion about the cessation of perception came up among them:
- **NOTE**: <i lang='pi' translate='no'>Abhisaññā</i> does not appear elsewhere. Here the prefix <i lang='pi' translate='no'>abhi-</i> means not “higher”, but rather “about, concerning”. Compare <i lang='pi' translate='no'>abhidhamma</i> at <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn32/en/sujato#8.6'>MN 32:8.6</a>: <i lang='pi' translate='no'>dve bhikkhū abhidhammakathaṁ kathenti</i> (“two mendicants engage in discussion about the teaching”); also <i lang='pi' translate='no'>abhivinaya</i> at <a href='https://suttacentral.net/an3.140/en/sujato#4.4'>AN 3.140:4.4</a>. The commentary here says <i lang='pi' translate='no'>abhi-</i> is a mere particle, so it need not be translated.

## dn9:6.5
- **PLI**: ‘kathaṁ nu kho, bho, abhisaññānirodho hotī’ti?
- **EN**: ‘How does the cessation of perception happen?’
- **NOTE**: This discussion appears to have been directly sparked by the difficult passage in Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 2.4.12 and 4.5.13. The sage Yājñavalkya, teaching his wife Maitreyī, says that the true Self is a sheer mass of “consciousness” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>vijñāna</i>, Pali <i lang='pi' translate='no'>viññāṇa</i>), which is “great, endless, infinite reality”. After realizing this, he says, there is no “perception” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>saṁjñā</i>, Pali <i lang='pi' translate='no'>saññā</i>), a statement that bewilders even the wise Maitreyī. He explains that only in an apparent state of duality (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>dvaitamiva</i>) do the separate functions of sense consciousness operate. When all is realized as the Self, how, he asks, can one know that owing to which all this is known? He is implicitly distinguishing between <i lang='pi' translate='no'>viññāna</i> as “infinite” (= <i lang='pi' translate='no'>vi-</i>) knowing and <i lang='pi' translate='no'>saññā</i> as “constrained” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>saṁ-</i>) knowing. Yājñavalkya says the separate Self emerges with these elements and vanishes with them (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>etebhyo bhūtebhyaḥ samutthāya tānyevānu vinaśyati</i>), but he does not explain how or why this happens, which is the question the theorists here attempt to address.

## dn9:6.6
- **PLI**: Tatrekacce evamāhaṁsu:
- **EN**: Some of them said:

## dn9:6.7
- **PLI**: ‘ahetū appaccayā purisassa saññā uppajjantipi nirujjhantipi.
- **EN**: ‘A person’s perceptions arise and cease without cause or reason.
- **NOTE**: Here perception is not identified with the “person” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>purisa</i>), but rather belongs to them (cf. <i lang='pi' translate='no'>etaṁ mama</i>, “this is mine”). In the discussion to follow, the Buddha only directly addresses this theory, while the remainder are included by inference.

## dn9:6.8
- **PLI**: Yasmiṁ samaye uppajjanti, saññī tasmiṁ samaye hoti.
- **EN**: When they arise, you become percipient.

## dn9:6.9
- **PLI**: Yasmiṁ samaye nirujjhanti, asaññī tasmiṁ samaye hotī’ti.
- **EN**: When they cease, you become non-percipient.’

## dn9:6.10
- **PLI**: Ittheke abhisaññānirodhaṁ paññapenti.
- **EN**: That’s how some describe the cessation of perception.

## dn9:6.13
- **PLI**: Saññā hi, bho, purisassa attā.
- **EN**: Perception is a person’s self,
- **NOTE**: The self is defined as perception (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>eso me attā</i>), one of the five aggregates. Compare the various theories of the self and perception at <a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn1/en/sujato#2.38.0'>DN 1:2.38.0</a>.

## dn9:6.14

- **EN**: which enters and departs.

## dn9:6.15
- **PLI**: Yasmiṁ samaye upeti, saññī tasmiṁ samaye hoti.
- **EN**: When it enters, you become percipient.

## dn9:6.16
- **PLI**: Yasmiṁ samaye apeti, asaññī tasmiṁ samaye hotī’ti.
- **EN**: When it departs, you become non-percipient.’
- **NOTE**: Implying that at such times a person lacks a “self”. This is perhaps related to Yājñavalkya’s: “That man, when born, acquiring a body, is connected with ills (the bodily organs); and when he dies, departing, he discards those ills” (Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 4.3.8).

- **EN**: There are ascetics and brahmins of great power and might.
- **NOTE**: The commentary says these were devotees of <i lang='pi' translate='no'>āthabbaṇa</i>, i.e the practices preserved in the Atharvaveda. This “fourth Veda” is mentioned only once by name in the early Pali (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/snp4.14/en/sujato#13.1'>Snp 4.14:13.1</a>), where, as here, it is associated with the performance of magic and the casting of spells. The commentary fairly drips with contempt: “Allegedly, the Āthabbaṇa practitioners cast a spell, showing a creature’s head as if cut off, or their hand as if cut off, or as if dead. Then they show them back to normal; imagining so, they say, ‘From cessation they have arisen.’”
