## dn14:1.1.1  `bj8.2, cck10.1, csp1ed7.1, csp2ed7.1, dr10.1, mc10.1, ms7D_2, msdiv1, ndp7.3, pts-cs1.1, pts-vp-pli2.1, sya10.1, vri2.1`
- **PLI**: Evaṁ me sutaṁ—
- **EN**: So I have heard.
- **NOTE**: In the suttas, <i lang='pi' translate='no'>apadāna</i> means “evidence”, “traces left behind” (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn27/en/sujato#16.4'>DN 27:16.4</a>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn24/en/sujato#2.7.3'>DN 24:2.7.3</a>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn15/en/sujato#3.32'>MN 15:3.32</a>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/an3.2/en/sujato#1.1'>AN 3.2:1.1</a>). The sense here seems to have been “(story about) the traces that deeds leave behind”. The sense “legend of past lives” probably grew out of this sutta, and later became the title of the collections of legends of past lives of the monks (Thera-apadāna) and nuns (Therī-apadāna).

## dn14:1.4.1  `cck10.3, ms7D_6, msdiv4, pts-cs1.4`

- **NOTE**: <i lang='pi' translate='no'>Sikhī</i> means “crested one” and refers to a halo or crown. A story of him and his disciples is told at <a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn6.14/en/sujato'>SN 6.14</a>; cf. <a href='https://suttacentral.net/an3.80/en/sujato'>AN 3.80</a>.

- **EN**: In the same thirty-first eon, the Buddha Vessabhū arose in the world, perfected and fully awakened.
- **NOTE**: <i lang='pi' translate='no'>Vessabhū</i> is Sanskrit Viśvabhṛt. It means “all-bearing”, probably originating as a word for the earth. A legendary king of the same name once ruled over the city of Avantī (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn19/en/sujato#36.14'>DN 19:36.14</a>).

## dn14:1.4.4
- **PLI**: Imasmiññeva kho, bhikkhave, bhaddakappe kakusandho bhagavā arahaṁ sammāsambuddho loke udapādi.
- **EN**: In the present fortunate eon, the Buddhas Kakusandha,
- **NOTE**: Due to the appearance of four Buddhas in this cycle it is known as “fortunate” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>bhadda</i>). | Stories from Kakusandha’s day appear at <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn50/en/sujato'>MN 50</a> and <a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn15.20/en/sujato#2.8'>SN 15.20:2.8</a>. There is no obvious etymology for his name, but perhaps it stems from <i lang='pi' translate='no'>kaku</i> (“peak”) and thus “the union of the peaks”, a valley at the junction of mountains.

## dn14:1.4.5
- **PLI**: Imasmiññeva kho, bhikkhave, bhaddakappe koṇāgamano bhagavā arahaṁ sammāsambuddho loke udapādi.
- **EN**: Koṇāgamana,
- **NOTE**: The meaning of Koṇāgamana is unclear. In Sanskrit it is often spelled Kanakamuni, “the golden sage”, while in the (dubious) Nigali Sagar Edict of Ashoka it is Konākamana. At <a href='https://suttacentral.net/thig16.1/en/sujato#71.1'>Thig 16.1:71.1</a> we hear the past life of three nuns in the time of Koṇāgamana.

## dn14:1.4.6
- **PLI**: Imasmiññeva kho, bhikkhave, bhaddakappe kassapo bhagavā arahaṁ sammāsambuddho loke udapādi.
- **EN**: and Kassapa arose in the world, perfected and fully awakened.
- **NOTE**: Kassapa (Sanskrit Kaśyapa) means “tortoise”. It is a common Brahmanical clan name, stemming from an ancient figure reckoned as the eldest of the “seven sages”, to whom some Vedic verses are attributed. Details of the Buddha Kassapa’s time are found at <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn81/en/sujato'>MN 81</a>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn15.20/en/sujato#4.1'>SN 15.20:4.1</a>, and <a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn48.57/en/sujato#3.1'>SN 48.57:3.1</a>.

## dn14:1.4.7
- **PLI**: Imasmiññeva kho, bhikkhave, bhaddakappe ahaṁ etarahi arahaṁ sammāsambuddho loke uppanno.
- **EN**: And in the present fortunate eon, I have arisen in the world, perfected and fully awakened.
- **NOTE**: These numbers make up a quasi-logarithmic scale: the time gaps multiply by three, while the number of Buddhas divides by two. In eon one there are four Buddhas. Thirty eons ago the number is halved, so they had two Buddhas. Twice-thirty eons before that, the number is halved again, to one Buddha, thus ending the scale.

## dn14:1.5.7
- **PLI**: Ahaṁ, bhikkhave, etarahi arahaṁ sammāsambuddho khattiyo jātiyā ahosiṁ, khattiyakule uppanno.
- **EN**: I was born as an aristocrat into an aristocrat family.
- **NOTE**: In ancient India, the “aristocrats” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>khattiya</i>) and the brahmins vied for the prestige of being the highest class. The traditional business of the aristocrats was land, politics, agriculture, and war, while the brahmins were a hereditary priestly caste who served as advisers and ritualists. It is said that the Buddha-to-be was born in whatever caste was most prestigious at the time so that his word would have the most impact. | In this sutta, the Buddha simply refers to himself as “I” with no personal name. When the seven Buddhas are listed at <a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn32/en/sujato#3.14'>DN 32:3.14</a>, however, he is called Aṅgīrasa.

## dn14:1.6.7
- **PLI**: Ahaṁ, bhikkhave, etarahi arahaṁ sammāsambuddho gotamo gottena ahosiṁ.
- **EN**: Gotama is my clan.
- **NOTE**: Gotama is an ancient Brahmanical clan name, which like Kassapa stems from the Vedas and the seven sages. Why does the Buddha, an aristocrat, have a Brahmanical clan? During initiation by a Brahmanical family priest (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>purohita</i>), an aristocrat was ritually determined to be a brahmin for a short time, during which he would assume the lineage name of the priest. After being restored as an aristocrat, he and his family would still be referred to by that name. Thus the Sakyans’ <i lang='pi' translate='no'>purohita</i> must have been of the Gotama lineage. Other examples of this practice include the Mallas who are called Vāseṭṭha (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn16/en/sujato#5.19.2'>DN 16:5.19.2</a>) and Saccaka who is called Aggivessana (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn35/en/sujato#4.2'>MN 35:4.2</a>). The Jain Ācārāṅgasūtra 2.15.15 records a similar situation, for while Mahāvīra was, like the Buddha, a <i lang='pi' translate='no'>khattiya</i>, he was of the Kāśyapa <i lang='pi' translate='no'>gotta</i>, and various relatives were assigned to Vāsiṣṭha, Kauṇḍinya, and Kauśika.
