## dn21:1.4.8  `ms7D_827, msdiv348`
- **PLI**: Ekamantaṁ ṭhito kho pañcasikho gandhabbadevaputto beluvapaṇḍuvīṇaṁ assāvesi, imā ca gāthā abhāsi buddhūpasañhitā dhammūpasañhitā saṅghūpasañhitā arahantūpasañhitā kāmūpasañhitā:
- **EN**: Standing to one side, Pañcasikha played his arched harp, and sang these verses on the Buddha, the teaching, the Saṅgha, the perfected ones, and sensual love.
- **NOTE**: The idea that the Buddha would be wooed by a love song seems like an absurd conceit, until you take into account the centaurs’ dangerous reputation as inveterate lechers. Pañcasikha wants to show that he is maturing and finally seeking a commitment in a respectable marriage.

## dn21:1.5.1  `ms7D_828, pts-cs1.5`
- **PLI**: “Vande te pitaraṁ bhadde,
- **EN**: “O Bhaddā Suriyavaccasā, my Darling Sunshine,
- **NOTE**: Suriyavaccasā (“Sunshine”) accords with the Vedic conception of the wife as the Sun (Sūryā, Rig Veda 10.85) who takes as husband Soma, the moon. She appears in Atharvaveda 8.10.27, which invokes the <i lang='pi' translate='no'>gandharvas</i> with their female counterpart the “nymphs” (Pali <i lang='pi' translate='no'>accharā</i>, Sanskrit <i lang='pi' translate='no'>apsaras</i>).

## dn21:1.5.2
- **PLI**: timbaruṁ sūriyavacchase;
- **EN**: I pay homage to your father Timbaru,
- **NOTE**: In Sanskrit literature, Timbaru (Sanskrit <i lang='pi' translate='no'>tumburu</i>, “coriander”) was an elder <i lang='pi' translate='no'>gandharva</i> musician in the courts of Indra and Kuvera. According to Rāmāyaṇa 3.4, he was cursed with a monstrous form by Kuvera due to an inappropriate lust for the nymph Rambhā. Upon his defeat at the hands of Rāma he was restored to his former status. Pañcasikha would have wanted to avoid a similar fate. Rather than Suriyavaccasā, the Sanskrit literature mentions his daughters Manuvantī and Sukeśī; the latter name (“fair-hair”) evokes the streaming rays of the sun (Vāyupurāṇa 69.49 = Brahmāṇḍapurāṇa 2.7.13).

## dn21:1.5.7
- **PLI**: Aṅgīrasi piyāmesi,
- **EN**: so dear are you, Aṅgīrasi, to me—
- **NOTE**: <i lang='pi' translate='no'>Aṅgīrasi</i> means “shining one” and is etymologically linked with <i lang='pi' translate='no'>agni</i> (“fire”). Suriyavaccasā earned the name due to both her radiant nature and her family connections: her father Timbaru was a son of Kaśyapa, who was (according to certain lineages) brother of Aṅgīras, from whom the Aṅgīrasas were descended. In the Arthavaveda—the only early non-Buddhist source for a nymph named Suriyavaccasā—<i lang='pi' translate='no'>aṅgīrasi</i> is associated with witchcraft (Atharvaveda 12.5.52). <i lang='pi' translate='no'>Aṅgīrasa</i> is also a patronymic of the Buddha (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn32/en/sujato#3.14'>DN 32:3.14</a>).

- **NOTE**: Pañcasikha is showing off his literary skill. The same image, the elephant, illustrates opposing qualities, peaceful and violent. For Pañcasikha, the elephant bursting its bonds illustrates his crazed devotion, whereas later it illustrates breaking free of desire (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn21/en/sujato#1.12.45'>DN 21:1.12.45</a>).
