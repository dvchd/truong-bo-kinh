## dn21:1.1.1  `bj8.396, cck10.315, csp1ed7.211, csp2ed7.211, dr10.294, mc10.226, ms7D_823, msdiv344, ndp7.197, pts-cs1.1, pts-vp-pli2.263, sya10.298, vri2.194`
- **PLI**: Evaṁ me sutaṁ—
- **EN**: So I have heard.
- **NOTE**: This late sutta exhibits considerable literary sophistication. The A-plot concerns Sakka’s rejection of his violent past and embrace of the Dhamma, while the B-plot concerns Pañcasikkha’s more modest growth from a libertine to a married man. This draws on such precedents as Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 3.2.4.4, where the centaur is urged to win his woman by seduction rather than force. | This discourse is quoted by name and a passage discussed at <a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn22.4/en/sujato'>SN 22.4</a>. In addition, the dialogue at <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn37/en/sujato'>MN 37</a> picks up directly where this leaves off and may be considered a sequel. A Sanskrit parallel published by Ernst Waldschmidt in 1979 is available on SuttaCentral as <a href='https://suttacentral.net/sf241/san/waldschmidt'>SF 241</a>. ## dn21:1.1.2

- **EN**: At one time the Buddha was staying in the land of the Magadhans, where east of Rājagaha there is a brahmin village named Ambasaṇḍā, north of which, on Mount Vediyaka, is Indra’s hill cave.

## dn21:1.2.1  `dr10.295, ms7D_824, msdiv345, pts-cs1.2, pts-vp-pli2.264, sya10.299`
- **PLI**: Atha kho sakko devānamindo pañcasikhaṁ gandhabbadevaputtaṁ āmantesi:
- **EN**: Then Sakka addressed the centaur Pañcasikha,
- **NOTE**: Introduced in a minor role in <a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn18/en/sujato'>DN 18</a>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn19/en/sujato'>DN 19</a>, and <a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn20/en/sujato'>DN 20</a>, Pañcasikha gets his moment to shine in this sutta. Outside of the Dīghanikāya, he appears only in <a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn35.119/en/sujato'>SN 35.119</a>. His name means “Fivecrest”, evidently in reference to his impressive hairdo; a nymph or group of nymphs bore the synonymous name Pañcacūḍā, “Five Topknots”. The centaur (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>gandhabba</i>) was a wild rogue of music, dancing, and sex. He must be tamed, and here this is achieved through the power of love and filial respect. A less diplomatic approach is taken in Atharvaveda 4.37.7, where the <i lang='pi' translate='no'>gandharva</i> with his “egg-crest” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>śikhaṇḍino</i>) is threatened with castration.

## dn21:1.2.2
- **PLI**: “ayaṁ, tāta pañcasikha, bhagavā magadhesu viharati pācīnato rājagahassa ambasaṇḍā nāma brāhmaṇagāmo, tassuttarato vediyake pabbate indasālaguhāyaṁ.
- **EN**: “Dear Pañcasikha, the Buddha is staying in the land of the Magadhans at Indra’s hill cave.

## dn21:1.2.3
- **PLI**: Yadi pana, tāta pañcasikha, mayaṁ taṁ bhagavantaṁ dassanāya upasaṅkameyyāma arahantaṁ sammāsambuddhan”ti?
- **EN**: What if we were to go and see that Blessed One, the perfected one, the fully awakened Buddha?”

## dn21:1.2.4
- **PLI**: “Evaṁ, bhaddantavā”ti kho pañcasikho gandhabbadevaputto sakkassa devānamindassa paṭissutvā beluvapaṇḍuvīṇaṁ ādāya sakkassa devānamindassa anucariyaṁ upāgami.
- **EN**: “Yes, lord,” replied the centaur Pañcasikha. Taking his arched harp made from the pale timber of wood-apple, he went as Sakka’s attendant.
- **NOTE**: The “arched harp” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>vīṇa</i>) was a stringed instrument held in the lap, whose form is clear from representations in ancient Indian sculptures in Bharhut, Amarāvatī, and Pawāyā, as well as the coins of Samudragupta. The strings, typically seven in number, were attached to the base of the soundbox and passed through holes in the parchment resonator to be tied with cords to the curved neck (these parts are listed at <a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn35.246/en/sujato#4.17'>SN 35.246:4.17</a>). It accompanied song or dance, and was played equally by men and women. It is not the modern Indian instrument called <em>veena</em>, which is a lute or stick zither, but rather is similar to the Burmese <em>saung</em>. | <i lang='pi' translate='no'>Gandhabbas</i> are closely associated with music (Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 3.2.4). Māra’s harp is described in the same way (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn4.23/en/sujato#10.7'>SN 4.23:10.7</a>), and tradition says they were in fact the same instrument.

## dn21:1.2.5  `bj8.398, cck10.316, csp1ed7.212, csp2ed7.212, mc10.227, ms7D_825, msdiv346, ndp7.198, vri2.195`
- **PLI**: Atha kho sakko devānamindo devehi tāvatiṁsehi parivuto pañcasikhena gandhabbadevaputtena purakkhato—
- **EN**: Then Sakka went at the head of a retinue consisting of the gods of the thirty-three and the godling centaur Pañcasikha.
- **NOTE**: In the Buddhist hierarchy of divinity, <i lang='pi' translate='no'>gandhabbas</i> occupy a more humble place than the gods of the thirty-three, yet here Pañcasikha appears with them in a position of honor. In <a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn19/en/sujato#28.3'>DN 19:28.3</a> we saw that the even more exalted Brahmā takes the form of Pañcasikha. This fluidity is characteristic of old Vedic cosmology, where there is no clear hierarchy of divinity. The formalized hierarchies in Buddhism and Hinduism are a later conception.

## dn21:1.2.6
- **PLI**: seyyathāpi nāma balavā puriso samiñjitaṁ vā bāhaṁ pasāreyya pasāritaṁ vā bāhaṁ samiñjeyya; evameva—devesu tāvatiṁsesu antarahito magadhesu pācīnato rājagahassa ambasaṇḍā nāma brāhmaṇagāmo, tassuttarato vediyake pabbate paccuṭṭhāsi.
- **EN**: As easily as a strong person would extend or contract their arm, he vanished from the heaven of the gods of the thirty-three and landed on Mount Vediyaka north of Ambasaṇḍā.

## dn21:1.4.2

- **EN**: “My dear Pañcasikha, it is hard for one like me to get near the Realized Ones while they are on retreat practicing absorption, enjoying absorption.
- **NOTE**: We will learn later of how Sakka’s appearance could be too disturbing for the Buddha while on retreat (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn21/en/sujato#1.10.13'>DN 21:1.10.13</a>). Note, however, that on subsequent occasions Sakka showed no such reticence, either because he was already a stream-enterer or because the Buddha was not in solitary retreat.
