# Mo dau  (4 đoạn)

## dn23:1.1  `bj8.504, cck10.368, csp1ed7.253, csp2ed7.253, dr10.349, mc10.270, ms7D_1090, msdiv406, ndp7.236, pts-cs1, pts-vp-pli2.316, sya10.352, vri2.237`
- **PLI**: Evaṁ me sutaṁ—
- **EN**: So I have heard.

## dn23:1.2
- **PLI**: ekaṁ samayaṁ āyasmā kumārakassapo kosalesu cārikaṁ caramāno mahatā bhikkhusaṅghena saddhiṁ pañcamattehi bhikkhusatehi yena setabyā nāma kosalānaṁ nagaraṁ tadavasari.
- **EN**: At one time Venerable Kassapa the Prince was wandering in the land of the Kosalans together with a large Saṅgha of five hundred mendicants when he arrived at a Kosalan citadel named Setavyā.
- **NOTE**: Kumārakassapa was ordained at twenty (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/pli-tv-kd1/en/sujato#75.1.1'>Kd 1:75.1.1</a>). He features in the Vammikasutta (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn23/en/sujato'>MN 23</a>), and his verses are collected in the Theragāthā (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/thag2.41/en/sujato'>Thag 2.41</a>). He was declared the foremost of those with brilliant speech (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/an1.217/en/sujato'>AN 1.217</a>), apparently on the basis of this discourse. | The Pāyāsisutta is the only major Buddhist text that has a Jain parallel, Paesi-kahāṇayaṁ, a similar dialogue between Paesi and Keśin in Setavyā.

## dn23:1.3
- **PLI**: Tatra sudaṁ āyasmā kumārakassapo setabyāyaṁ viharati uttarena setabyaṁ siṁsapāvane.
- **EN**: He stayed in the rosewood grove to the north of Setavyā.
- **NOTE**: Setavyā was north-east of Sāvatthī.

## dn23:1.4
- **PLI**: Tena kho pana samayena pāyāsi rājañño setabyaṁ ajjhāvasati sattussadaṁ satiṇakaṭṭhodakaṁ sadhaññaṁ rājabhoggaṁ raññā pasenadinā kosalena dinnaṁ rājadāyaṁ brahmadeyyaṁ.
- **EN**: Now at that time the chieftain Pāyāsi was living in Setavyā. It was a crown property given by King Pasenadi of Kosala, teeming with living creatures, full of hay, wood, water, and grain, a royal park endowed to a brahmin.
- **NOTE**: Pāyāsi was a <i lang='pi' translate='no'>khattiya</i> yet he receives a <i lang='pi' translate='no'>brahmadeyya</i>. The sense of <i lang='pi' translate='no'>brahmadeyya</i> as a donation to brahmins is well attested in inscriptions, so I think this is likely a mistake in the text, rather than evidence that the practice was not for brahmins only.
