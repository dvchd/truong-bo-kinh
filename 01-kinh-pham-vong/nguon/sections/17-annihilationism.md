- **PLI**: 3.2.4. Ucchedavāda 
- **EN**: 3.2.4. Annihilationism 

## dn1:3.9.1  `bj7.60, csp1ed6.31, csp2ed6.31, km14.81, ms6D_157, msdiv84, ndp6.30, pts-cs3.9, pts-vp-pli1.34`
- **PLI**: Santi, bhikkhave, eke samaṇabrāhmaṇā ucchedavādā sato sattassa ucchedaṁ vināsaṁ vibhavaṁ paññapenti sattahi vatthūhi. 
- **EN**: There are some ascetics and brahmins who are annihilationists. They assert the annihilation, eradication, and nonexistence of an existing being on seven grounds. 
- **NOTE**: These theorists assert the true existence of a being, thus falling into the fallacy of identity view. For the Buddha, the words “being” or a “self” describe an ongoing process that is conditioned and impermanent, and do not correspond to a genuine metaphysical reality. The distinction between contingent, empirical reality and metaphysical, absolute existence is essential to understanding early Buddhism.

- **EN**: And what are the seven grounds on which they rely?

- **EN**: ‘This self is formed, made up of the four principal states, and produced by mother and father. Since it’s annihilated and destroyed when the body breaks up, and doesn’t exist after death, that’s how this self becomes rightly annihilated.’ 
- **NOTE**: This is the materialist view, which accepts only the coarse physical realm. This view is common today, but was also well known in the Buddha’s time. | The “four principal states” are earth, water, fire, and air, i.e. the states of matter: solid, liquid, plasma, and gas.

- **EN**: ‘<em>That</em> self of which you speak does exist, I don’t deny it. 
- **NOTE**: The theorist accepts multiple selves. As self theories evolve, they typically move from more coarse materialist theories toward more subtle conceptions. Sometimes the former view is rejected as being false. Sometimes, as here, the former view is seen not as false, but as incomplete and shallow.

## dn1:3.11.4
- **PLI**: Atthi kho, bho, añño attā dibbo rūpī kāmāvacaro kabaḷīkārāhārabhakkho. 
- **EN**: There is another self that is heavenly, formed, sensual, consuming edible food. 
- **NOTE**: “Form” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>rūpa</i>) includes not just the physical realm of the elements, but various kinds of subtle form (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>sukhumarūpa</i>). These include the energetic or mind-made bodies of beings in various dimensions. <i lang='pi' translate='no'>Rūpa</i>  ultimately refers to the appearance or manifestation of physical properties, and can even include the perception of colors, lights, and shapes in the mind. Here the bodies of the divine beings are not very distant from our own, as they still consume solid food. This probably refers to various nature deities or entities that were believed to consume the food offered to them by humans.

- **EN**: You don’t know or see that. 

## dn1:3.11.6

- **EN**: But I know it and see it. 

## dn1:3.11.7
- **PLI**: So kho, bho, attā yato kāyassa bhedā ucchijjati vinassati, na hoti paraṁ maraṇā, ettāvatā kho, bho, ayaṁ attā sammā samucchinno hotī’ti. 
- **EN**: Since this self is annihilated and destroyed when the body breaks up, and doesn’t exist after death, that’s how this self becomes rightly annihilated.’ 
- **NOTE**: Whereas the eternalists believe that their heavenly rebirth will last forever, annihilationists believe that even heaven is limited, and it ends in final annihilation.
