# Muc 23 — Describing the Self  (23 đoạn)

## dn15:23.0
- **PLI**: 2. Attapaññatti
- **EN**: 2. Describing the Self

## dn15:23.1  `cck10.81, ms7D_220, msdiv117, pts-cs23, vri2.50`
- **PLI**: Kittāvatā ca, ānanda, attānaṁ paññapento paññapeti?
- **EN**: How do those who describe the self describe it?
- **NOTE**: The text now turns to an analysis of theories of “self” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>attā</i>), which is comparable to some of the passages of <a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn1/en/sujato'>DN 1</a>. The Buddha began his discourse by stating that it is the failure to understand dependent origination that keeps beings trapped in transmigration. Dependent origination explains transmigration in a purely empirical way by inferring from the mental and physical phenomena we experience here and now. Self theorists, on the other hand, explain transmigration by introducing a new metaphysical principle, the “self” or “soul”, by which they assume that the individual has an eternal underlying essence.

## dn15:23.2
- **PLI**: Rūpiṁ vā hi, ānanda, parittaṁ attānaṁ paññapento paññapeti:
- **EN**: They describe it as formed and limited:
- **NOTE**: “Formed” is <i lang='pi' translate='no'>rūpī</i> (“possessing form”), identifying the self with the first of the five aggregates. If something were really the core essence of a person, you would think it is readily knowable. But the Buddha shows that theorists describe the self in multiple different and incompatible ways. Each of these draws on some more-or-less arbitrary aspect of empirical reality, such as “form”, to describe an unknowable metaphysical entity that is in fact just pure supposition.

## dn15:23.3
- **PLI**: ‘rūpī me paritto attā’ti.
- **EN**: ‘My self is formed and limited.’
- **NOTE**: An example of a self that is “physical and limited” would be the body.

## dn15:24.1  `dr10.76, mc10.57, ms7D_221, msdiv118, pts-cs24`
- **PLI**: Tatrānanda, yo so rūpiṁ parittaṁ attānaṁ paññapento paññapeti.
- **EN**: Now, take those who describe the self as formed and limited.

## dn15:24.2
- **PLI**: Etarahi vā so rūpiṁ parittaṁ attānaṁ paññapento paññapeti, tattha bhāviṁ vā so rūpiṁ parittaṁ attānaṁ paññapento paññapeti, ‘atathaṁ vā pana santaṁ tathattāya upakappessāmī’ti iti vā panassa hoti.
- **EN**: They describe the self in the present as formed and limited; or they describe it as sure to be in some other place formed and limited; or else they think: ‘Though it is not like that, I will ensure it is provided with what it needs to become like that.’
- **NOTE**: The three options (“it is”, “it will be so”, and “I will make it be so”) illustrate how the theorist resorts to ever more convoluted means to justify the lack of empirical support for their pet theory. | <i lang='pi' translate='no'>Bhāviṁ</i> (“sure to become”) is the root <i lang='pi' translate='no'>bhū</i> with the primary affix <i lang='pi' translate='no'>-ī</i>, which connotes an inevitable future state. | <i lang='pi' translate='no'>Tattha</i> (“in some other place”, literally “there”) is explained by the commentary as <i lang='pi' translate='no'>paraloke</i> (“in the next world”).

## dn15:24.3
- **PLI**: Evaṁ santaṁ kho, ānanda, rūpiṁ parittattānudiṭṭhi anusetīti iccālaṁ vacanāya.
- **EN**: This being so, it’s appropriate to say that a theory of self as formed and limited underlies them.
- **NOTE**: Their surface differences rest on the same underlying assumption, so if the assumption is disproved there is no need to refute each individual theory. | <i lang='pi' translate='no'>Iccālaṁ</i> resolves to <i lang='pi' translate='no'>iti alaṁ</i>.
